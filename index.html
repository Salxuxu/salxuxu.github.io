The challenge

The organization made sense internally. It did less to help coaches find a tool.

Resources were grouped by department, while coaches were looking for ways to complete a task. A dense grid gave tools similar visual weight, leaving users to read through labels to decide where to go.

The design problem was to make a large collection easier to navigate: clearer entry points, a stronger hierarchy, and consistent interactions across the experience.

Discovery → direction

Research changed what the interface needed to prioritize.

12 coach interviews

Coaches described difficulty finding resources relevant to their workflows and a preference for task-based organization.

Design implication

Use the coach’s task as the starting point for navigation.

300+ resources audited

The audit mapped existing content against user needs and identified redundant resources and gaps.

Design implication

Clarify the structure before polishing individual tiles.

Interface review

Inconsistent patterns and weak visual hierarchy made the collection harder to scan and navigate.

Design implication

Give labels, actions, and reusable components consistent roles.

Decision 01 · Information architecture

Organize around tasks coaches recognize.

I reorganized a department-based collection into a workflow-aligned structure, giving users a clearer way to choose where to begin.

Before

Start with the organization

  1. Choose an internal department
  2. Scan a uniform set of resource tiles
  3. Read labels to find a relevant tool
After

Start with the work

  1. Identify the task or workflow
  2. Scan clearly labeled resources
  3. Use the relevant tool’s action

Conceptual summary of the change in organization, rather than a reproduction of the original navigation.

Decision 02 · Hierarchy

Make the next action easier to identify.

The old grid asked users to inspect every tile. I introduced clearer labels, consistent spacing, and stronger visual hierarchy to help coaches distinguish resources and their actions.

This connected the information architecture to the interface: organizing content was only useful if people could scan it and decide what to do next.

Decision 03 · UI systems

Build familiarity across Focal Point tools.

I aligned typography, icons, and interaction patterns with the FP brand and the updated Library. The aim was a consistent experience as coaches moved between platforms.

A shared visual language also gave the Resource Center a repeatable way to present tools, instead of treating every resource as a separate design problem.

My contribution

From discovery to the implemented experience.

As Lead UX Designer, I connected the research findings to the resource structure and interface, then translated the high-fidelity Figma design into Wix with responsive behavior across desktop and mobile.

  • Conducted discovery interviews with 12 coaches.
  • Audited and mapped 300+ existing resources.
  • Defined workflow-aligned resource organization.
  • Designed hierarchy, navigation, and high-fidelity UI in Figma.
  • Implemented the redesigned experience in Wix.

Coach feedback

A clearer experience for everyday resource discovery.

What coaches told us

Coaches described the redesigned Resource Center as easier to use, with clearer pathways to the tools they rely on daily.

Summary of qualitative coach feedback, not a measured percentage change.

Reflection & next steps

Make the structure visible in the interface.

This project connected two layers of the same problem: how resources were organized and how coaches could recognize them on screen. The redesign needed both task-aligned architecture and a consistent hierarchy.

What I would validate next

I would compare success and time-on-task for common resource-finding scenarios, then examine where newer and experienced coaches still take different paths. That would help distinguish improvements in visual clarity from improvements in task completion.
